Run 1

Prepare your land for fire season by clearing dry vegetation and creating protective buffer zones before the peak summer heat begins.

When to Prepare

  • Winter and Early Spring: Start heavy tasks like removing dead trees, cutting thick brush, and pruning lower branches when the weather is cool and wet.
  • Late Spring: Finish clearing dry grass, clean gutters, and establish your fire breaks before high temperatures arrive.
  • Summer: Maintain short grass and monitor the property daily for new dry debris or hazards.

How to Prepare

  • Zone 1 (0 to 2 meters from buildings): Remove all dead leaves, dry grass, and flammable mulch. Use gravel or stone instead of wood chips. Move firewood piles and outdoor furniture away from exterior walls.
  • Zone 2 (2 to 10 meters): Cut grass to under 10 centimeters. Trim tree branches up to 2 meters from the ground to eliminate "ladder fuels" that let fire climb into the canopy. Space out shrubs and trees.
  • Zone 3 (10 to 30 meters): Thin dense clusters of trees and create clear fire breaks like gravel paths or wide driveways.

With What Tools and Materials

  • Hand Tools: Use loppers, hand saws, and rakes for clearing dead leaves, pine needles, and low twigs.
  • Power Equipment: Use a lawn mower or brush cutter for grass, and a chainsaw or pole saw for limbing trees and removing heavy brush.
  • Protective Materials: Install non-combustible ground covers like gravel or stone, metal mesh screens (1/8-inch) on vents and gutters to block embers, and keep operational water hoses or a filled water tank ready if you live in a high-risk rural zone.

If you share what kind of terrain or vegetation you have (pine forest, open grass, steep slopes), I can give you more specific rules for your land.

Run 2

To prepare your land for fire season, create defensible space by clearing dry vegetation, removing ladder fuels, and maintaining a safe zone around your structures.

Immediate Zone (0 to 5 feet from structures)

  • Clear Debris: Remove dead leaves, pine needles, and twigs from your roof, gutters, and underneath decks.
  • Remove Flammables: Store firewood, lumber, and other combustible materials far away from exterior walls.
  • Use Non-Combustible Materials: Replace flammable mulch near your foundation with gravel, stone, or concrete.

Intermediate Zone (5 to 30 feet from structures)

  • Mow Grass: Keep lawns and native grasses trimmed short (under 4 inches or 10 cm).
  • Prune Trees: Cut lower tree branches up to 6 to 10 feet from the ground to eliminate "ladder fuels" that allow surface fires to climb into the canopy.
  • Space Vegetation: Group shrubs and plants into small, separated islands rather than continuous beds.

Extended Zone (30+ feet to property lines)

  • Clear Dead Growth: Remove dead trees, heavy underbrush, and dry fallen timber.
  • Thin Canopies: Maintain space between tree crowns so fire cannot easily jump from tree to tree.
  • Know Local Rules: Check specific regional requirements for mandated clearing radiuses and legal deadlines through local resources like A Minha Terra.

Run 1

Fire risk is high in the bioregion, so preparation is essential. The traditional knowledge that persists among older farmers and shepherds here includes fire use and avoidance practices that limit catastrophic burns — so connecting with neighbors who've managed land long-term is valuable.

For practical preparation: clear dead wood and dense shrub growth, especially on slopes where fire moves fastest. If you're in or near montado (mixed woodland with grazing), maintain that open structure through regular, low-intensity grazing — historically this reduces large fire risk. Create defensible space around buildings and along access routes by removing fuel within 10–15 meters.

Timing matters most before the hot, dry months typically June through September, so aim to finish clearing by late May. For water security during fire season, check your cistern and well capacity now — the reservoir is at only 36–37% capacity region-wide, so having stored water for firefighting and livestock is critical.

If your land has fragile shallow soils on schist (common here), avoid heavy machinery that destabilizes slopes. Instead, use manual clearing or light grazing. And if you're considering any land-use changes, know that the region's fire risk is elevated on shrub-encroached areas, so keeping vegetation managed is as important as how you manage it.

Run 2

Given that fire risk is high in Odemira and climate change is extending droughts and elevating wildfire risk on shrub-encroached areas, your main priorities are fuel management and access. Clear dead wood, thin dense shrub growth, and maintain firebreaks around your property—especially important if your land has schist slopes prone to erosion, since exposed soil after fire compounds that damage. Traditional grazing regimes have historically kept montado systems open and reduced large fire risk, so if you have access to livestock, controlled grazing to reduce ground fuel can be effective. Create or maintain cisterns and wells for water harvesting—not only for drought resilience, but also to have water available for emergency response. Keep access roads clear so fire services can reach your land quickly. The water in reservoirs is at critical levels (36–37% of capacity), so don't rely on external water supply during a fire event.

Run 1

If you're replacing eucalyptus, cork oak (Quercus suber) and holm oak (Quercus rotundifolia) are the natural choices — they're the foundation of montado, the emblematic semi-natural agroforestry system across the Alentejo. Cork oak is legally protected as a priority habitat and has been recognized since the 16th century in this region, so you're also supporting conservation.

Beyond oaks, the shrublands and heathlands on poorer soils here naturally support Mediterranean flora adapted to dry conditions — species like rockrose, lavender, and other maquis plants that thrive without intensive inputs. If you want productive diversity, consider agroforestry: combine oak with legume understory for nitrogen fixation, or integrate grazing for livestock and land management that also reduces fire risk through open structure.

Your soil matters — if it's shallow schist (common inland), shallow-rooted systems work better than deep competitors. And given rising temperatures and longer droughts ahead, focus on drought-resilient natives rather than anything water-hungry.

Talk with farmers managing montado nearby; they have generations of knowledge about what thrives on your specific slope and soil, and about markets for cork and acorns.

Run 2

If you're replacing eucalyptus on inland slopes, cork oak (Quercus suber) or holm oak (Quercus rotundifolia) in a montado system is the obvious choice—it's the emblematic Alentejo agroforestry model, provides long-term cork income, supports grazing, and is a legally protected priority habitat recognized since the 16th century. On poorer soils where montado isn't viable, native shrublands and Mediterranean heathland species are ecologically appropriate and support the ~750 plant species and diverse bird life the region depends on. If you're on steeper coastal terrain, native cliff-top scrub and dune grasslands restore rare natural Mediterranean coastal landscape. The key advantage of moving away from eucalyptus is reducing fuel load for wildfires—eucalyptus burns intensely and regenerates aggressively, whereas cork oak and native Mediterranean vegetation are far more fire-resilient. If you want commercial or food production, olive groves fit the agricultural mosaic pattern across the region and are drought-tolerant given the longer droughts ahead.
